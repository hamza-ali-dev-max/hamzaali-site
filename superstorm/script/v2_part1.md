## 1 · T+0 — The Flare (1:30–7:00)

### Boulder, USA · 04:12 local · T+00:00 {B1}

[World map, sunlit half. HUD boots: MISSION CLOCK T+00:00:00 · X-RAY FLUX. Zoom to Colorado.
PiP {B1}: the operations room, a forecaster stands up.]

NARR: At 11:12 in the morning, universal time, a knot of magnetic field on the Sun — fifteen times the width of the Earth — snaps.
FORECASTER: Are you seeing this? That's not an X10. That's off the scale.
NARR: The instruments that measure solar flares top out. The needle simply stays pinned at the maximum.
NARR: And because that flash is light, it doesn't warn anyone. Eight minutes after it leaves the Sun, it's already here.

### North Atlantic · 11:20 UTC {B2}

[Map: the day side of the planet flushes red — HF RADIO BLACKOUT. A jet icon over the ocean.
Radio panel: OCEANIC CONTROL — HF. PiP {B2}: the cockpit.]

PILOT: Gander, Speedbird two-one-two... Gander, do you read? ...Nothing on any frequency.
NARR: Over the oceans, long-range flights talk to the ground by shortwave radio. For the next few hours, on the whole sunlit side of the planet, shortwave doesn't work.
NARR: The X-rays have supercharged the upper atmosphere. Instead of bouncing radio signals back down to Earth, it swallows them.

### Lagos, Nigeria · 12:25 local

[Map to West Africa, zoom into Lagos (map only). Caption: HF RADIO BLACKOUT ACROSS THE DAY SIDE.
Sound: a harbour radio, static.]

NARR: Eight minutes after the flare, its X-rays hit the day side of the planet. Every shortwave radio from Lagos to Delhi goes silent.
NARR: Ships' radio operators, rescue coordinators, military networks — all of them switch to satellites. For now, the satellites still work.

### Low Earth orbit · 410 km · T+01:28 {B3}

[Map: the orbital station as a cyan dot crossing Africa. HUD: PROTON FLUX RISING · S4.
Full screen {B3}: the crew moves into the shielded module.]

FLIGHT: Station, Houston... get everyone into the shielded module. Now, please.
NARR: Behind the light comes the first wave of particles — protons, fast enough to cross from the Sun in under an hour.
NARR: On the orbital station, the crew packs into the one module with the thickest walls, and waits. Outside, a day's worth of radiation arrives in minutes.

### Boulder, USA · 05:30 local · T+01:18

[Coronagraph panel: a white halo balloons out around a black disc. HUD: CME SPEED 2,900 KM/S ·
ETA T+17:00, the countdown starts.]

NARR: Then the coronagraph images arrive — the ones that matter.
NARR: A halo. A cloud of magnetised plasma, a billion tons of it, spreading out in every direction at once. When it looks like a ring around the Sun, it means one thing: it's coming straight at us.
FORECASTER: Twenty-nine hundred kilometres a second. That's... seventeen hours. Call the director. And call the grid operators.
NARR: In 1859, the biggest solar storm ever recorded took seventeen and a half hours to arrive. It set telegraph offices on fire and painted auroras over the Caribbean.
NARR: In 1859, the world had no power grid.

## 2 · The Warning (7:00–14:00)

### Tokyo, Japan · 21:00 local · T+00:48 {T2} {T3}

[Map east across Asia to Japan; zoom into Tokyo (plain night map). {T2} hands over from the
zoom's last frame to the real city: towers, expressways, the crossing. PiP {T3}: the crowd stops
under the giant screens. Caption: SOLAR STORM WARNING ISSUED.]

NARR: The first country to take it seriously is the one that plans for disasters every day.
TOKYO: 巨大な太陽嵐が地球に向かっています。到達は明日の午後の予想です。
*(A massive solar storm is heading for Earth. It's expected to arrive tomorrow afternoon.)*
NARR: In Tokyo it's nine at night. Thirty-seven million people are going home. The storm will reach them tomorrow — in broad daylight, when the Sun is overhead.

### London, United Kingdom · 13:00 local · T+01:48

[Map to Britain, zoom into London (map). Radio panel: GRID CONTROL — PRESS BRIEFING. Headline
card, Northern Ledger: "Grid operator: 'prepare for loss of supply'".]

GRID_UK: We are preparing for the possibility of widespread loss of supply tonight. Please don't panic-buy.
NARR: Britain's grid is long and thin — power lines running hundreds of kilometres north to south. That's exactly the shape that catches the most current from a geomagnetic storm.
NARR: The grid operator knows it. The briefing lasts four minutes. Nobody asks a question they want the answer to.

### Mumbai, India · 18:00 local · T+01:18

[Map to India, zoom into Mumbai (map). Phone-alert graphic pulses across the subcontinent:
EMERGENCY ALERT ON 400 M PHONES.]

NARR: In Mumbai, four hundred million phones buzz at once. Most people swipe it away.
NARR: India is close to the equator, where the Earth's magnetic field is strongest at shielding the ground. Its grid will bend tonight, but its biggest problems will come later — from everything it imports and everything it's connected to.

### Anchorage · the polar routes · T+02:48

[Map: great-circle flight arcs over the Arctic slide south one by one. Caption: POLAR ROUTES
CLOSED · 312 FLIGHTS REROUTED.]

NARR: Airlines pull every flight off the polar routes. Up there, above the magnetic poles, the radiation dose on a single flight is now a real number.
NARR: Three hundred and twelve flights take the long way round. It's the last thing that will go smoothly for a while.

### Orbit · T+03:48

[Map: thousands of cyan satellite dots; waves of them turn amber: SAFE MODE. Counter:
SATELLITES IN SAFE MODE: 1,840.]

NARR: Satellite operators turn thousands of spacecraft edge-on to the storm, switch off their instruments, and wait.
NARR: Weather satellites, communications, navigation. Everything we see the planet through is about to go partially blind.

### New York, USA · 07:40 local · T+01:28 {N1}

[Map to the US east coast, zoom into New York (map). PiP {N1}: the queue outside the hardware
store. Caption: GENERATORS SOLD OUT BY 10 A.M.]

VOX_NY: Batteries, flashlights, whatever they got. My building's forty floors, man.
NARR: New York wakes up to the news. By ten in the morning, there isn't a portable generator left for sale in the five boroughs.
NARR: The people who buy them tend to live in houses. The people who need them live forty floors up, above pumps that stop when the power does.

### Montreal, Canada · 09:00 local · −9 °C

[Map up to Quebec, zoom into Montreal (map, snowy dawn). Caption: PROPANE: SOLD OUT.
Sound: a hardware store, a cash register, wind.]

VOX_MTL: Il reste plus rien. Plus de propane, plus de piles. Rien.
*(There's nothing left. No propane, no batteries. Nothing.)*
NARR: Montreal knows this story better than anywhere. On the thirteenth of March, 1989, a solar storm a fraction of this size took down the whole Quebec grid in ninety seconds. Six million people, in the dark, in winter.
NARR: The grid was hardened after that. The question tonight is whether it was hardened enough.
