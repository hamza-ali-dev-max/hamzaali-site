# Script — Part 2: The Waiting → Impact → The First Night (14:00–33:00)

## Chapter 3 — The Waiting (14:00–20:00)

**14:00 | Montreal afternoon aerial, long shadows, traffic.** `[FULL: aerial winter city afternoon]` HUD countdown 08:40:00.

**NARR**:
> At 2 p.m., new data arrives from a spacecraft parked a million and a half kilometres upstream — our early-warning buoy in the solar wind.
> It gives about forty minutes' notice before a storm hits. But even from here, the forecasters can already see what's rushing toward it.

**14:40 | `[PiP: LENA and team crowding one monitor]`**

**LENA**:
> Speed's been revised. Thirty-one hundred. Arrival moves up… sixteen hours fifty from launch.
> *(beat)* And I don't like the magnetic field models. If this thing arrives pointing south…

**NARR**:
> That's the detail that decides everything. A storm's magnetic field can point north or south.
> Pointing north, Earth's field shrugs most of it off. Pointing south, it links up — and the storm pours straight in.
> Nobody will know which way it points until forty minutes before impact.

**15:40 | Montreal store PiPs, arrows to three points: queues outside a big-box store; a man loading a generator into a pickup; empty shelves of water bottles.** `[PiP ×3]`

**NARR**:
> Montreal does what cities do. The shelves empty in a predictable order: batteries, water, candles, propane.
> The last generator in the city sells at a hardware store in Laval at 3:47 p.m. The buyer paid four times the sticker price — to a stranger in the parking lot.

**16:30 | Hospital exterior at dusk `[PiP: hospital entrance, staff wheeling diesel drums]`. Radio panel: "ST-LAURENT GENERAL — FACILITIES".**

**FACILITIES** (radio, calm):
> Generator test complete, all three units run. We have seventy-two hours of diesel on site. Resupply contract says another delivery within twenty-four hours of an outage.

**NARR**:
> Seventy-two hours. It's a number chosen for ice storms and blizzards — events that end.
> Hold on to it.

**17:15 | World map at dusk; the terminator reaches the Americas. HUD: countdown 05:10:00. Europe goes to night.**

**NARR**:
> In London, it's nearly midnight. In Stockholm, people are already standing in the snow, waiting for a light show.
> In Tokyo, it's the middle of the night before; the storm will reach them in the early afternoon, in full daylight.

**18:00 | Montreal, night. `[PiP: rooftop "aurora party", people with blankets, phones up, laughing]`.**

**VOX** (EN, excited): It's supposed to be the best show in, like, a hundred and fifty years! We've got hot chocolate!

**18:40 | T−00:40:00. The upstream spacecraft reading flips on the HUD: Bz −48 nT (red).**

**LENA** `[PiP]` (flat, quiet):
> Bz just turned south. Hard south.
> *(beat)* Call everyone. It's going to be bad.

**19:20 | Countdown under ten minutes. Everything slows. Drone. The map pushes in on the whole planet.**

**NARR**:
> At 11:02 p.m. in Montreal, the countdown has eight minutes left.
> The grid is carrying a normal Tuesday night: heating, streetlights, hospitals, trains, four million phones charging on four million nightstands.

## Chapter 4 — T+17: Impact (20:00–26:00) — *scene 1, extended*

**20:00 | Scene-1 opening: world map, HUD T+16:59:56 → 17:00:00, aurora crossfade, IMPACT.**

**NARR**:
> Hour seventeen. Right on schedule, the storm arrives. Across the northern sky, auroras flare brighter than anyone alive has ever seen.

**20:12 | Zoom: world → Quebec (beat) → Montreal.**

**NARR**:
> And the first place to feel it… is Quebec.

**20:20 | Seedance aerial from the zoom's last frame: the city at night under a green sky, lights on.** `[FULL: omni-reference, start image = zoom end frame]`

**NARR**:
> Its power grid runs across ancient bedrock that can't absorb the surge. In 1989, a storm a fraction of this size took this grid down in ninety seconds.
> Tonight… it takes nine.

**20:31 | Blackout wave: districts die one by one, flickers, a few hospitals stay lit, red outline traces the island, green aurora tint. HUD: PEOPLE WITHOUT POWER 0 → 9,000,000. Hum → thunk → 2 s silence.**

**20:42 | Radio panel: GRID CONTROL — CH 4.**

**GRID** (tense, fast):
> Grid Control to all stations… we've lost the northern lines. Multiple transformer trips… Montreal is down. I repeat, we are losing the network… we are losing the— *(static)*

**20:50 | `[FULL: GNN reporter, downtown street in darkness, phone flashlights, green-magenta sky]` Lower third: GNN · LIVE · MONTREAL. 4 s crowd shot cut in.**

**GNN MONTREAL** (EN):
> I'm in downtown Montreal, where just a minute ago every light in the city went out at once. People are pouring into the streets… and above us, you can see it, the whole sky is green. We're hearing the entire province may be without power.

**21:05 | Pull-back to the world map; Quebec's outline pulses red.**

**NARR**:
> Nine million people. In the dark. And the storm is only getting started.

**21:15 | The cascade. Map stays wide; red outlines draw themselves in sequence with a sting each: New York & New England (T+17:04), Ontario (T+17:06), the UK (T+17:09), Scandinavia (T+17:12). HUD PEOPLE WITHOUT POWER: 9 M → 31 M → 58 M → 94 M.**

**NARR**:
> When Quebec's grid drops, its neighbours lose three hundred megawatts of imports in an instant. Frequencies sag. Protection relays do exactly what they're designed to do — they disconnect.
> The American Northeast falls four minutes later. Ontario two minutes after that.
> Across the Atlantic, it's four in the morning. Britain's long north-south transmission lines light up with induced current. Then Scandinavia — the same ancient rock as Quebec.

**22:30 | `[PiP ×2 at once: London reporter on a dark street (EN, British); Stockholm vox-pop under aurora (EN subtitles from Swedish)]`.**

**LONDON REPORTER**:
> The lights went out across most of the country at about ten past four. What you can hear is… nothing. No traffic. Just people outside, looking up.

**23:10 | Satellites: several cyan dots on the map turn red and stop moving: CONTACT LOST.**

**NARR**:
> In orbit, the storm heats the upper atmosphere and it swells — dragging on low satellites like a sudden headwind. Some lose control of their orientation. Some go silent.
> GPS is now off by tens of metres. For some receivers, it simply stops.

**24:00 | Tokyo, 1 p.m. daylight. `[PiP: Tokyo office, lights flicker, workers look up]` TOKYO reporter on the phone.**

**TOKYO** (JA):
> 北海道と東北の一部で停電が発生しています。関東の電力網は今のところ持ちこたえています。
> *(Blackouts are being reported in parts of Hokkaido and Tohoku. The Kanto grid is holding for now.)*

**NARR**:
> Japan's grid bends but mostly holds. Shorter lines, different rock, and a lot of luck.
> Which makes the next hours even stranger. Half the planet is watching the other half go dark… live, on the internet.
> For as long as the internet lasts.

**25:30 | Hook: undersea cables appear on the map as thin cyan lines across the oceans. One starts blinking amber.**

## Chapter 5 — The First Night (26:00–33:00)

**26:00 | Montreal, 11:40 p.m. Aerial of the dark city, aurora overhead, headlights crawling.** `[FULL: dark city aerial, only car headlights moving]` HUD: OUTSIDE TEMP −12 °C.

**NARR**:
> Forty minutes after the blackout, Montreal is minus twelve degrees and falling.
> Every traffic light is dark. The metro stopped between stations — thousands of people are walking out through the tunnels with their phone lights.

**26:45 | `[PiP: metro tunnel, people walking single file with phone lights]` + `[PiP: intersection chaos, two cars touching bumpers, horns]`.**

**NARR**:
> Elevators stopped too. Fire crews spend the night going floor to floor — there are two thousand elevators in downtown Montreal alone.

**27:30 | Radio panel: 9-1-1 DISPATCH.**

**DISPATCH** (FR/EN, overwhelmed):
> Unité douze, on a un appel pour une personne coincée… *(Unit twelve, we've got a call for someone trapped…)* — all units, call volume is fourteen times normal, prioritise life-threatening only—

**28:10 | Map: cell towers as tiny amber dots across Quebec; they fade out in waves. HUD: MOBILE NETWORK 100% → 62% → 18%.**

**NARR**:
> Cell towers have battery backup. Usually four to eight hours.
> Around 3 a.m., they start dying — one after another. The last thing most people see on their phones is a map of the blackout, still loading.

**29:10 | Undersea cables: the cable lines on the map blink red, segment by segment. HUD: INTERNET — TRANSATLANTIC CAPACITY −71%.**

**NARR**:
> On the ocean floor, internet cables are fine — glass doesn't care about magnetism. But every eighty kilometres or so there's a repeater: a small box of electronics that boosts the signal, powered through the cable itself.
> The storm induces thousands of volts along those power lines. Repeater by repeater, the transatlantic cables go dark.

**30:15 | Hospital: the lit building in the dark city (from the blackout shot). `[PiP: ICU corridor, generator lights, nurses]`. Radio panel: ST-LAURENT GENERAL.**

**NURSE** (radio, calm but tired):
> We're on generator. ICU is stable. We've moved dialysis to the morning. We need the fuel truck confirmed.

**NARR**:
> The hospitals with generators are islands of light. And people see it. By 2 a.m., the emergency waiting rooms are full of people who aren't sick — just cold, and scared, and looking for somewhere with power.

**31:20 | Japan, early evening: `[PiP: Tokyo street, crowds watching a giant screen showing the dark Atlantic coast]`.**

**TOKYO** (JA):
> ヨーロッパと北米の東部が暗闇に包まれています。東京からは、その様子をリアルタイムで見ることができます。
> *(Europe and eastern North America are shrouded in darkness. From Tokyo, we can watch it in real time.)*

**32:15 | ESTIMATED DEATHS counter appears on the HUD at 0 and ticks to 3.**

**NARR**:
> The first deaths of the storm aren't dramatic. A car crash at a dark intersection. A man on a home oxygen machine, whose battery ran out at 4 a.m. An elderly woman who fell on the stairs in the dark.
> Nobody counts them as storm deaths yet.
> By the end of the week, everyone will.
