# Script — Part 1: Bonjour Montréal → The Flare → The Warning (0:00–14:00)

Format per beat: `TIME | VISUAL` then lines. Speakers: **NARR** (English documentary
narrator), **ANIM** (Montreal morning-radio host, French, EN subtitles), **LENA** (Dr. Lena
Varga, duty forecaster, fictional North Atlantic Space Weather Centre), **GNN** anchors and
reporters (fictional network), **TOKYO** (reporter for fictional Kanto News 24, Japanese,
EN subtitles), **GRID** (Grid Control dispatcher), vox-pops. All names and outlets are fictional.
`[PiP: …]` = Seedance clip in a picture-in-picture box with an arrow to the map location.
`[FULL: …]` = full-screen Seedance clip. Audio direction in *italics*.

## Chapter 0 — Bonjour Montréal (0:00–1:30)

**0:00 | Black. A radio dial sweeps through static.** *Station jingle, three soft notes.*

**ANIM** (warm, upbeat, fast Québécois morning energy):
> Bonjour Montréal! Il est six heures pile, vous écoutez Radio Boréale 99,3.
> *(Good morning Montreal! It's six o'clock sharp, you're listening to Radio Boréale 99.3.)*

**0:06 | Dawn world map, gold lights, the day/night line creeping over the Atlantic. Slow push toward North America.**

**ANIM**:
> Moins neuf degrés ce matin, un ciel clair, et ça bouchonne déjà sur le pont Champlain.
> *(Minus nine this morning, clear skies, and traffic's already backed up on the Champlain bridge.)*
> Petite nouvelle sympathique : les scientifiques disent qu'on pourrait voir des aurores boréales ce soir. Alors sortez vos tuques, levez les yeux!
> *(Fun little story: scientists say we might see northern lights tonight. So grab your toques and look up!)*

**0:24 | Zoom Quebec → Montreal (the scene-1 zoom, in daylight-to-dawn colours). Seedance hands off into an aerial of Montreal at dawn: steam from rooftops, traffic on the bridges, the river.** `[FULL: aerial dawn city]`

**NARR** (calm, low, slightly slow):
> This is Montreal. Four million people, waking up on an ordinary Tuesday in March.
> They don't know it yet — but ninety-three million miles away, the Sun has just made a decision.

**0:48 | Cut to the Sun: a slow, heavy close-up of the solar surface, an active region twisting.** `[FULL: sun close-up, no text]`

**NARR**:
> For three days, a knot of magnetic field the size of fifteen Earths has been twisting on the Sun's surface. Tangling. Storing energy.
> At 6:12 this morning, Eastern time… it snaps.

**1:05 | White flash. The HUD boots up line by line: MISSION CLOCK T+00:00:00 · X-RAY FLUX · CME SPEED — · KP 2.0.** *Sub-bass hit, then a rising electrical whine.*

**NARR**:
> What happens next is the most documented disaster that has never happened.
> Everything you're about to see is a simulation — built from real physics, real power grids, and what happened the last time the Sun did this.

**1:30 | TITLE: I SIMULATED A HYPER REALISTIC SOLAR SUPERSTORM — DAY BY DAY** *(HUD font, glitch-in, one hard drone hit.)*

## Chapter 1 — T+0: The Flare (1:45–6:00)

**1:45 | World map. The sunlit half of the planet flushes red: HF RADIO BLACKOUT. Counter: X-RAY FLUX climbs past X10, X20… the bar runs off the scale and blinks.**

**NARR**:
> Light travels from the Sun in eight minutes. So the first wave of this storm doesn't warn anyone. It just arrives.
> Across the entire sunlit side of the planet, high-frequency radio goes silent.

**2:05 | Radio panel: "OCEANIC CONTROL — HF 8891". Map: a jet icon over the Atlantic.** *Radio hiss.*

**PILOT** (calm, clipped, over radio FX):
> Gander, Speedbird four-one-one, position report… Gander, how do you read? …Gander?

**NARR**:
> Pilots over the Atlantic lose contact with the ground. GPS positions start drifting — first by metres, then by tens of metres.

**2:25 | `[PiP: space-weather operations room, two forecasters at glowing screens, night shift, one leans in]` arrow → fictional centre near Boulder (label: N. ATLANTIC SPACE WEATHER CENTRE).**

**LENA** (quietly, then sharper):
> Okay… that's an X-class. That's a big one.
> …No. Wait. Pull the other channel.
> *(beat)* That's not an X10. That's off the scale. The sensors are saturated.

**CO-WORKER** (off, nervous laugh):
> So what is it, X20?

**LENA**:
> I don't know. Nobody's ever measured one this big with instruments.

**3:05 | Coronagraph-style panel: a white halo balloons out in every direction around a black disc.** `[PiP: stylised coronagraph, halo CME]`

**NARR**:
> Twenty minutes later, the coronagraphs show the thing that actually matters.
> A halo. A cloud of magnetised plasma — billions of tons of it — expanding in every direction at once.
> When a halo looks like a ring around the Sun, it means one thing: it's coming straight at us.

**3:35 | HUD: CME SPEED 2,900 km/s · ETA 17:00:00 → countdown starts ticking.**

**LENA** (on the phone, fast, controlled):
> Speed estimate is twenty-nine hundred kilometres a second. That puts arrival at… seventeen hours. Maybe less.
> I need the director. And I need someone to call the grid operators. Now.

**NARR**:
> For comparison: in 1859, the biggest solar storm ever recorded took seventeen and a half hours to cross the gap.
> It set telegraph offices on fire. Operators got electric shocks from their own equipment. Auroras were seen in Cuba.
> In 1859, the world had no power grid. Tonight, it will.

**4:25 | History card: sepia-tinted 1859 telegraph office illustration (Seedance, no text) inside a PiP; HUD card "CARRINGTON EVENT · 1 SEP 1859".**

**4:45 | Map: satellites as small cyan dots in orbit bands; one by one they flash amber: SAFE MODE.**

**NARR**:
> Satellite operators start putting spacecraft into safe mode — shutting down instruments, turning away from the Sun, waiting.
> Astronauts on the orbital station are ordered into the most shielded module on board. Because behind the flare comes a storm of protons, fast enough to cross the gap in under an hour.

**5:20 | HUD adds: PROTON STORM S4.**

**NARR**:
> By 7 a.m. in Montreal, fewer than a hundred people on Earth understand what's coming.
> In the next two hours, that number will reach eight billion.

## Chapter 2 — The Warning (6:00–14:00)

**6:00 | World map, daylight Americas. News spreads: PiP boxes pop up with arrows, one after another (New York, Montreal, London, Tokyo). Headline cards slide in.**

**GNN ANCHOR** `[PiP: news anchor at a desk, blue studio, New York]`:
> Breaking news this morning: space-weather forecasters are warning that a massive solar eruption could hit Earth tonight. Officials are urging calm — but they're also urging people to prepare.

**Headline card:** THE NORTHERN LEDGER — "Largest solar flare ever measured; storm expected tonight"

**6:30 | Montreal. `[PiP: reporter outside a hardware store at morning, people carrying boxes]`**

**GNN MONTREAL** (French, EN subtitles):
> Ici à Montréal, les gens n'ont pas attendu : à neuf heures, il n'y avait déjà plus une seule génératrice sur les tablettes.
> *(Here in Montreal, people didn't wait: by nine o'clock there wasn't a single generator left on the shelves.)*

**NARR**:
> Quebec has a reason to listen. On March 13th, 1989, a storm much weaker than this one hit the province.
> The grid collapsed in ninety seconds. Six million people lost power for nine hours — in the middle of a Canadian winter.

**7:15 | Map zooms to Quebec; long transmission lines draw themselves from the far north down to Montreal (animated gold lines). Bedrock texture overlay: "CANADIAN SHIELD — HIGH RESISTIVITY".**

**NARR**:
> Here's why. Most of Quebec's electricity is made hundreds of kilometres to the north, at giant dams — and carried south on some of the longest high-voltage lines in the world.
> Those lines sit on ancient bedrock that barely conducts electricity. When the Earth's magnetic field starts shaking, current in the ground has nowhere to go…
> …so it goes into the power lines. Straight into the transformers.

**8:05 | Diagram (HUD style): a transformer icon; a slow DC current creeps in, the core glows, heat rises.**

**NARR**:
> A large power transformer can weigh four hundred tons. It's custom-built. There are only a handful of factories on Earth that make them, and a new one takes between one and two years to deliver.
> Remember that. It's going to matter.

**8:40 | ~5 minutes in: TOKYO. Map swings east across the Pacific (fast pan, whoosh). Evening in Japan. `[PiP: female reporter on a busy Tokyo street at night, neon, umbrellas, crowds]`**

**TOKYO** (Japanese, measured, EN subtitles):
> 本日午後八時、史上最大規模の太陽フレアが観測されました。専門家によりますと、明日の午後、日本にも影響が出る可能性があるということです。
> *(At 8 p.m. today, the largest solar flare ever recorded was observed. According to experts, Japan could also be affected tomorrow afternoon.)*
> 政府は、不要不急の外出を控え、非常用の水と電池を準備するよう呼びかけています。
> *(The government is asking people to avoid unnecessary travel and to prepare emergency water and batteries.)*

**NARR**:
> Japan will face the storm in daylight. No auroras overhead — but the currents in the ground don't care what time it is.

**9:30 | Map: polar flight routes (thin arcs over the Arctic) slide south one by one. HUD: FLIGHTS REROUTED 1,240.**

**NARR**:
> Airlines pull every flight off the polar routes. Ships switch to paper charts. Stock markets open — and the utilities crash eleven percent in the first ten minutes.

**10:05 | Radio panel: GRID CONTROL — CH 1 (briefing, calm).**

**GRID** (steady, professional):
> All stations, this is Grid Control. Storm protocol is in effect as of now. We are reducing transfers on the northern corridor to seventy percent and bringing every reserve unit online. Next update at twelve hundred.

**NARR**:
> Grid operators do the only thing they can: run the network gently, keep spare capacity ready, and hope the transformers can take the heat.

**10:45 | Montreal street vox-pops, quick cuts inside PiP boxes around the city (arrows to three neighbourhoods).**

**VOX 1** (FR, amused): *C'est des aurores boréales, là. On va avoir un beau spectacle, c'est tout.* *(It's northern lights. We're getting a nice show, that's all.)*
**VOX 2** (EN, stressed, carrying water): I've got two kids and a baby. I'm not taking any chances.
**VOX 3** (FR, laughing): *Mon beau-frère a acheté trois génératrices. Trois!* *(My brother-in-law bought three generators. Three!)*

**11:30 | Headline cards stack: "Hospitals test backup generators" · "Carmakers pause production over chip fears" · "Aurora parties planned across Canada".**

**NARR**:
> By noon, the whole world is watching the same number on the same countdown.
> Some people are stockpiling. Some people are planning parties. And somewhere in between, a lot of people are doing what humans always do before a storm.
> They're telling themselves it'll probably be fine.

**12:30 | Map: the day/night terminator sweeps west across the Atlantic. Europe's lights come on. HUD countdown: 10:48:00.**

**LENA** `[PiP: the forecaster, tired, coffee]`:
> The worst thing about this job? You can see it coming. You can tell everyone exactly when it'll hit. And there's almost nothing anyone can do to stop it.

**13:30 | Hook: slow push toward Quebec; HUD countdown ticks; a low pulse builds.**

**NARR**:
> Ten hours to go.
> And the storm is getting faster.
