## 1. Verdict: ARGUABLE, with a real path to yes

EI regular benefits after a voluntary quit require "just cause" under section 29(c) of the Employment Insurance Act. The legal test, confirmed in the Digest of Benefit Entitlement Principles Chapter 6: just cause exists where, "having regard to all the circumstances," the claimant had **"no reasonable alternative to leaving."** The burden of proof is on you, on a balance of probabilities.

Your facts map onto three recognized circumstances:

| Your fact | Statutory circumstance | Strength |
|-----------|----------------------|----------|
| Regular unpaid hours (floor work after your duties, no overtime pay) | s.29(c)(viii): "excessive overtime work or **refusal to pay for overtime work**" | **Strongest.** The Digest states such refusals "often violate the provisions in collective agreements or labour standards legislation" |
| Working hours without pay at all (if any unpaid hours were within a normal 8-hour day) | s.29(c)(xi): "practices of an employer that are contrary to law" | **Strong.** BC's Employment Standards Act requires pay for all hours worked. The Digest: just cause exists "where such practices or activities were proven or where the person honestly believed that the employer's activities were illegal" |
| Promised raise never delivered; meetings dodged | Not squarely any listed item | **Weak on its own**, but the dodged meetings are important evidence for the "no reasonable alternative" test (see below) |
| Paid $22.50/hr while the same seat paid $30 in 2025 and $25 in 2024 | Not a listed circumstance | **Not just cause by itself.** Dissatisfaction with wage level is not in the s.29(c) list. s.29(c)(vii) covers "significant modification of terms and conditions respecting wages or salary," meaning a change to YOUR agreed wage, which did not happen |

**The hinge is "no reasonable alternative."** The Digest is explicit that unpaid-overtime failures constitute just cause "only where a person first attempted all reasonable alternatives for remedying the situation." Service Canada will ask: did you raise it with the employer, did you demand the wages in writing, did you complain to the BC Employment Standards Branch, did you look for other work before quitting?

- In your favour: you repeatedly sought meetings to resolve pay and they were dodged; a raise was promised around June 15 and not delivered; you have a dated log of unpaid hours. That is a documented pattern of attempting reasonable alternatives and the employer frustrating them.
- Against you: you had not filed an Employment Standards complaint before resigning, and you quit without other employment lined up (s.29(c)(vi), "reasonable assurance of another employment," is the cleanest quit route and you do not have it). Adjudicators regularly deny wage-dissatisfaction quits on exactly this gap.

**Move that materially strengthens the claim: file the BC Employment Standards complaint for the unpaid hours NOW, before or at the same time as the EI application.** It converts "he says the employer broke the law" into "a complaint is on file with the provincial regulator," it proves the honest belief the Digest requires for s.29(c)(xi), and it costs nothing. Note the honest sequencing: the complaint being filed after resignation is fine; the ESB accepts complaints up to 6 months after the last day of work.

Bottom line: not a slam dunk, plausibly winnable, and worth applying. Answer truthfully; a denial costs nothing further, and you can appeal (reconsideration, then the Social Security Tribunal). If denied, the only lasting effect is that the ~11 months of insurable hours from HPP cannot fund a regular-benefit claim; hours from any new job start counting immediately.

## 4. BC Employment Standards rules confirmed current (via gov.bc.ca search results)

- **Overtime:** time-and-a-half for time worked over 8 hours in a day (up to 12), double time over 12 hours in a day; time-and-a-half over 40 hours in a week, with only the first 8 hours of each day counting toward the weekly total. Confirmed from the Province of BC hours-of-work and overtime-pay pages.
- **Complaint window:** complaint must be filed within 6 months of the last day of work (ESA Part 10, s.74); the director may extend in special circumstances.
- **Wage recovery window:** wages that became payable in the **12 months** before the complaint date (or last day of employment) are recoverable (ESA Part 10, s.80). Your ~11 months at HPP fall entirely within the window if you file promptly.

## 5. 2026 rule changes found

- **No change to voluntary-quit / just-cause rules.** Every 2026 source checked (Ascent Employment Law's 2026 EI summary, the Canada Gazette amendments, canada.ca temporary-measures page) leaves the s.29(c) just-cause framework untouched. The standard rule remains: quit without just cause means no regular benefits on those hours.
- **Waiting period waived (applies to you):** the one-week waiting period is waived for new claims starting between March 30, 2025 and **October 10, 2026** per the canada.ca temporary-measures page as summarized in search results. If approved, you would be paid from week one.

One discrepancy to verify on the live page: Canada Gazette SOR/2026-64 (published April 8, 2026) extended the pilot measures to April 11, 2026, while the canada.ca temporary-measures page shows the further extension to October 10-11, 2026. The later canada.ca date appears current but could not be page-verified from this machine.
- **20 extra weeks for long-tenured workers: does NOT apply to you.** It requires paying at least 30% of the maximum annual EI premium in 7 of the last 10 years; you have roughly one year of Canadian insurable employment.
- **2026 rates:** maximum insurable earnings $68,900; maximum weekly benefit $729; premium rate $1.63 per $100 (outside Quebec). At $22.50/hr full time, your benefit would be roughly 55% of average insurable weekly earnings, approximately $495/week before tax, subject to Service Canada's calculation.
